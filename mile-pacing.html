How to pace a mile

Four quarters, one purposeful mile: run the first lap with control, keep the middle honest, and leave yourself a genuine finish.

Published 16 October 20269 min readRace strategy + research

The quick answer

  1. 0–409m: Accelerate into position, then commit to a realistic mile rhythm. Race adrenaline can make the first 200m feel effortless, but a huge first-lap time gain is rarely free.
  2. 409–809m: Keep cadence economical and settle behind a useful group or maintain your own pace. Avoid running too wide or continually overtaking to recover a second.
  3. 809–1209m: The third lap can feel the longest mentally. Keep concentration on breathing, upright posture and manageable segments; do not let an unplanned gap open.
  4. 1209–1409m: At the bell, make an honest assessment. In races for places, stay in contact with the leading runners before the pace lifts. With 200m remaining, start progressively committing.
  5. 1409m–finish: Focus down the straight, use your arms and finish through the line. You may be holding off a slowdown rather than producing the fastest 200m of the race.

What the pacing research says

WHAT WE KNOW · AND WHAT WE DON'T

A published review summarising 32 mile world records reports that in 90% of those performances the slowest lap was either the second (34%) or third (56%). The fastest lap was usually the first or last. That describes world-record races, not a formula that makes a runner faster by deliberately slowing mid-race.

The mile is 1609.344m, not exactly 1600m. Four 400m laps leave another 9.344m. A four-quarter-mile view uses 402.336m per quarter, so splits need to be labelled clearly. For a personal best, distribute the effort steadily with a finish; for tactical racing, position and changes of pace can become more important than perfectly even quarter times.

What elite racing can teach us

Hicham El Guerrouj set his 3:43.13 mile world record in Rome in 1999. World Athletics reported quarter-mile (440-yard) marks of 55.07, 1:51.58 and 2:47.91, with a 55.22-second final quarter. These are official reported quarter-mile splits, not 400m lap splits.

Common pacing mistakes

  • Spending too much early: An unsustainable first lap can cost more than it gains.
  • Confusing increasing effort with increasing speed: Fatigue can rise even when the lap split is unchanged.
  • Losing position by accident: In a tactical race, a perfect lap time means little if you are boxed in for the decisive move.
  • Waiting too long to race: Build commitment before the last few strides, without turning the third lap into an all-out sprint.

Questions runners ask

Quick answers to common questions about pacing a Mile race.

Is a mile exactly four laps?

Not on a standard 400m outdoor track. A mile is 1609.344m, so a complete mile adds 9.344m to 1600m; the start line is staggered to account for this.

Should I bank time in the first lap?

Usually avoid an excessive first lap. Small pace changes are normal, but opening far faster than your sustainable speed can compromise the finish.

Why does the third lap feel so hard?

It is the moment when fatigue grows while the finish remains distant. Use rhythm and posture cues rather than waiting for a dramatic surge.
