800M RACE GUIDE · ATHLETE-INFORMED

How to pace an 800m

Four 200-metre sections. A fast-but-controlled opening. A patient middle. Then the commitment to hold your speed when it hurts. Here’s how to put together an 800m that lasts the full two laps.

Published 16 October 202610 min readTrack / 2 lapsResearch + race examples

The quick answer

  1. 0–200m: Get out decisively, find position and merge after the breakline without wasting energy.
  2. 200–400m: Settle into smooth, fast running. Relax your face and shoulders; don’t force the pace.
  3. 400–600m: Reassess your position at the bell. With 300m remaining, progressively increase effort and stay engaged.
  4. 600–800m: Commit at 200m to go, protect your technique and give everything in the final 100m.

The 800m, broken into four 200s

The following framework comes from the experience of a competitive middle-distance runner, refined against race analysis and published evidence. It gives you mental jobs for every quarter of the race without pretending every 800m should follow exactly the same pace curve. Championship races can demand very different decisions from paced time trials.

01 / 0–200m

Strong start. Smart position.

Accelerate positively, but don't sprint blindly. Get into a useful position while staying relaxed enough to keep moving well. In a standard two-lap 800m, stay in your lane until the marked breakline after the first bend, then merge smoothly towards the inside if it is safe. Taking the shortest line helps, but forcing a gap or getting boxed in can cost you more.

Cue: “Fast, comfortable, controlled.”
02 / 200–400m

Find flow. Don't force it.

Once the field settles, stop fighting the clock and find your rhythm. Keep your stride quick and fluid, the arms efficient, and your upper body relaxed. Resist small surges caused only by anxiety. You can run hard without feeling as if you are forcing every stride.

Cue: “Smooth and tall.”
03 / 400–600m

Prepare, then squeeze.

At the bell, take a quick inventory: are you trapped, on someone's shoulder, or positioned to move? Don't launch an all-out sprint simply because the bell has rung. With about 300m remaining, start building your commitment and defending your position. Make tactical decisions early enough that you're not forced into a desperate wide move.

Cue: “Stay in touch. Start squeezing.”
04 / 600–800m

Gear five. Then gear six.

At 200m to go, commit. Increase your effort, keep the arms active and resist the tendency to tense up. With 100m left, give what remains. Your speed may still fall; the practical goal is often to fade less than the runners around you.

Cue: “Hold form. Fight for every metre.”

One crucial distinction: “Change gears” is a mental instruction, not a guarantee of increasing speed. Research on elite championship middle-distance races suggests that successful athletes often differentiate themselves over the final 200m by slowing less, rather than accelerating dramatically. [3]

Should an 800m be positively split?

Usually, a small positive split is a reasonable starting point for a fast, time-focused 800m: the first 400m is slightly quicker than the second. One athlete-informed target is a second lap approximately 2% slower than the first, but this is an illustrative strategy, not an established physiological optimum.

Observational evidence offers context. A study of 142 elite season-best 800m performances reported that the men's first 200m was the fastest segment and subsequent segments slowed significantly, while women showed a different pattern. A broader review likewise notes that positive pacing is typical of paced 800m meetings, whereas championship races may be positive or negative depending on tactics. [1, 2]

What Rudisha's world record really shows

In the 2012 Olympic final, David Rudisha front-ran a world record of 1:40.91. His official intermediate marks were 49.28 at 400m and 1:14.30 at 600m; race reporting records an opening 200m of approximately 23.4. That produces the approximate segment profile below. [4]

There is no one-to-one link between Rudisha's record splits and the ideal plan for an athlete running 1:55 or 2:20. What the race illustrates is decisive positioning and exceptional speed endurance: a very quick first 200m, continued pressure from 400–600m and a final segment that, despite enormous effort, was slower than his opening section.

What about Coe and Wottle?

Sebastian Coe ran 1:41.73 for an 800m world record in 1981, with approximately 49.7 and 52.1-second laps. Like Rudisha, his record run used a sizeable positive split. Those exceptional performances provide useful reference points, but they don't prove that every runner should go out four or five percent faster. [5]

Dave Wottle's 1972 Olympic victory provides a different perspective. Wottle finished in 1:45.86 after running close to even 200m segments and catching runners who had gone out faster. In a later interview, he explained that his approach was not a rigidly pre-planned trick: it reflected his conditioning, mindset and a relatively tactical race. [6]

Time trial versus championship: If your priority is a personal best with a pacemaker, controlled positive splitting may make sense. If you're racing to qualify or win, changing pace to stay out of trouble or cover a move may matter more than following exact lap targets. [2, 3]

Why the final 200m feels so hard

The 800m demands very high running speeds for long enough that fatigue builds rapidly. People often blame lactic acid for the heavy-legged sensation, but this description isn't scientifically accurate. Lactate isn't simply toxic waste: it can be used as a fuel and it is not the direct, singular cause of fatigue.

Loughborough University exercise physiologist Dr Mark Burnley has explained that fatigue during events such as the 800m and mile involves multiple high-intensity metabolic factors, including inorganic phosphate. The practical point remains: early pace affects how well you can maintain force, coordination and form later. [7]

Three mistakes to avoid

  • Going out like it's a 400m: a fast start is not permission to sprint uncontrollably. Arrive at 200m in a position you can sustain.
  • Checking out from 200 to 500m: relaxing your body is good; losing contact or drifting unnecessarily is not.
  • Waiting until the straight to make a decision: many races are shaped from 500–650m. Anticipate changes rather than reacting once a gap has formed.

Make a plan, then adapt to the race

Use the split explorer to choose a realistic opening lap, and then rehearse your four race cues in training. Know where your target pace should put you through 200m and halfway—but don't treat those numbers as an instruction to ignore the runners around you.

For a race with a pacemaker, focus on staying relaxed within your plan. For an untimed championship, position and the ability to react matter. In both situations, the best finish usually starts with not wasting energy in the opening 400m.

800m pacing questions

Should I run an 800m even or positive splits?

Many fast 800m performances use positive splits, but tactical races can be different. Start with a controlled plan based on your own training and adjust to the situation; no single percentage is universally optimal.

Should I kick at 300m or 200m to go?

At about 300m to go, assess position and begin increasing commitment. The final 200m is often about protecting form and resisting deceleration; a major early sprint can backfire if you have too little left.

When can you move into lane 1 in an 800m?

In a standard two-lap track 800m started in lanes, athletes typically break after the first bend at the designated breakline. Always follow the markings and rules for the specific race format. [8]

How to pace a 1500m

A great 1500m asks for courage without recklessness: get up to pace, protect your third lap, and have a plan for the final 300m.

Published 16 October 20269 min readRace strategy + research

The quick answer

  1. 0–300m: Accelerate cleanly from the gun, secure space without unnecessary jostling, and settle into your planned rhythm. Your opening 300m should feel assertive, not like a sprint.
  2. 300–700m: Check effort against your goal pace. Relax jaw, shoulders and hands; conserve energy by running a sensible line. If the race is tactical, stay close enough to respond without repeatedly darting around runners.
  3. 700–1100m: This section often feels much harder despite unchanged split times. Keep cadence and posture purposeful, avoid drifting behind the pack and resist an unnecessary panic surge. Around the bell, think about position and a clear path.
  4. 1100–1300m: With 400m left, be aware of the runners ahead and your route through the field. Progressively raise your commitment rather than waiting passively for the final bend.
  5. 1300–1500m: With 200m remaining, use the arms, drive towards the line and maintain form. If speed does not rise, fighting the slowdown can still make the difference. The final 100m is for giving everything you have.

What the pacing research says

WHAT WE KNOW · AND WHAT WE DON'T

A 2021 analysis of 37 historical men’s 1500m world records found a broadly U-shaped pattern: the first lap and final 300m tended to be faster than the middle two laps. This is observational evidence from exceptional male performances, not proof that every runner should copy the pattern.

A study of 16 men’s European, World and Olympic 1500m championship races found many more tactical surges in slower races than fast ones. The finishing kick generally began around 240m from the line, while the timing of earlier decisive moves varied. For a time-focused effort, aim for controlled, relatively even rhythm; in a tactical race, positioning and responses to surges matter.

What elite racing can teach us

Hicham El Guerrouj ran 3:26.00 in Rome in 1998. Published race footage reports approximately 55.0 at 400m, 1:50.73 at 800m and 2:46.34 at 1200m, followed by 39.66 for the final 300m. The resulting first 400m, second 400m, third 400m and last 300m were approximately 55.0, 55.73, 55.61 and 39.66 seconds; these are different-length segments, so the last 300m should not be compared directly to a 400m lap.

Common pacing mistakes

  • Spending too much early: An unsustainable first lap can cost more than it gains.
  • Confusing increasing effort with increasing speed: Fatigue can rise even when the lap split is unchanged.
  • Losing position by accident: In a tactical race, a perfect lap time means little if you are boxed in for the decisive move.
  • Waiting too long to race: Build commitment before the last few strides, without turning the third lap into an all-out sprint.

Questions runners ask

Does every 1500m need a fast last lap?

No. Fast record attempts tend to finish strongly, but tactical races can slow and surge. Treat the final 300m as a cue for increasing commitment, not a prescribed faster split.

How should I pace the third lap?

Try to protect your rhythm rather than forcing an all-out move. Effort can rise even when the stopwatch stays steady.

Is even pacing wrong for a personal best?

No. A realistic, fairly steady target pace is a useful baseline. A slight early acceleration and late push can happen without deliberately sprinting the opening lap.

How to pace a mile

Four quarters, one purposeful mile: run the first lap with control, keep the middle honest, and leave yourself a genuine finish.

Published 16 October 20269 min readRace strategy + research

The quick answer

  1. 0–402m: Accelerate into position, then commit to a realistic mile rhythm. Race adrenaline can make the first 200m feel effortless, but a huge first-lap time gain is rarely free.
  2. 402–805m: Keep cadence economical and settle behind a useful group or maintain your own pace. Avoid running too wide or continually overtaking to recover a second.
  3. 805–1207m: The third lap can feel the longest mentally. Keep concentration on breathing, upright posture and manageable segments; do not let an unplanned gap open.
  4. 1207–1409m: At the bell, make an honest assessment. In races for places, stay in contact with the leading runners before the pace lifts. With 200m remaining, start progressively committing.
  5. 1409m–finish: Focus down the straight, use your arms and finish through the line. You may be holding off a slowdown rather than producing the fastest 200m of the race.

What the pacing research says

WHAT WE KNOW · AND WHAT WE DON'T

A published review summarising 32 mile world records reports that in 90% of those performances the slowest lap was either the second (34%) or third (56%). The fastest lap was usually the first or last. That describes world-record races, not a formula that makes a runner faster by deliberately slowing mid-race.

The mile is 1609.344m, not exactly 1600m. Four 400m laps leave another 9.344m. A four-quarter-mile view uses 402.336m per quarter, so splits need to be labelled clearly. For a personal best, distribute the effort steadily with a finish; for tactical racing, position and changes of pace can become more important than perfectly even quarter times.

Is a mile exactly four laps?

Not on a standard 400m outdoor track. A mile is 1609.344m, so a complete mile adds 9.344m to 1600m; the start line is staggered to account for this.

Usually avoid an excessive first lap. Small pace changes are normal, but opening far faster than your sustainable speed can compromise the finish.

5K PACING GUIDE · RESEARCH + RACE STRATEGY

How to pace a 5K

A controlled opening, an honest middle, and a committed finish. Learn how to distribute effort through 5km without wasting your race in the first kilometre.

Published 16 October 20268 min readTrack & roadResearch-backed

The quick answer

  1. 0–1km: Accelerate positively, but settle close to your realistic target pace by the first few hundred metres. The aim is to avoid a large early pace spike.
  2. 1–2km: Relax your hands and shoulders, use economical strides, and avoid surges to cover a few positions. Keep the pace honest, not desperate.
  3. 2–3km: This middle kilometre can feel mentally long. Stay focused on form and breathing; don’t mistake rising effort for a need to slow.
  4. 3–4km: Reassess your position and energy. If you feel capable, gradually increase pressure without committing to a full sprint too early.
  5. 4–5km: Stay strong through the penultimate bend or last road turns. With 400–600m left, let the effort rise; use the last 200m for whatever kick remains.

What does the research actually show?

In analyses of historical men’s 5000m world records, the first and final kilometres tended to be quicker than the middle sections, while much of the race remained relatively steady. That is a description of world-record behaviour, not proof that recreational runners should start faster than target pace.

Championship 5000m finals can be very different. A peer-reviewed analysis of official Olympic and World Championship splits found fluctuating speed and fast closing phases among medallists and other high finishers. A sensible personal-best plan may therefore look steadier than a championship-winning plan.

THE EVIDENCE IN CONTEXT

World-record pacing and championship racing do not look identical. The published literature describes broadly steady kilometre splits with an end spurt in record races, but championship finals are more variable and often accelerate sharply late. Choose the plan that suits your actual goal.

Review: Pacing profiles and tactical behaviors of elite runners ↗

Learning from elite racing

Joshua Cheptegei, Monaco 2020. His then-world-record 5000m of 12:35.36 offers a compelling example of rhythm: World Athletics reports 1000m in 2:31.87, 2000m in 5:03.77, 3000m in 7:35.14 and a slightly quicker 2:30 fourth kilometre before a powerful final kilometre and 59.64-second final lap. The lesson is disciplined pacing with a reserve for the finish, not blindly replicating elite speeds.

Mistakes that cost runners time

  • Turning the opening 400m into a sprint; the adrenaline can hide an unsustainable pace.
  • Chasing a watch reading on tight bends or under trees; use course markers and perceived effort.
  • Treating the first difficult kilometre as failure and giving up before the finish.
  • Waiting until the final 100m to commit when you still have energy left.

Common 5K pacing questions

Is even pacing best for a 5K?

Even pace is a useful starting point for a personal-best attempt on a relatively flat course. A small controlled quickening late in the race is often preferable to banking time early; championship tactics can be different.

Should my first kilometre be my fastest?

Not deliberately. An opening kilometre that is slightly quick can occur, but accelerating far beyond sustainable pace creates avoidable risk. Aim to settle into the effort you can hold.

When should I kick in a 5K?

There is no universal distance. If you still have control at 4km, build pressure gradually; a committed final 400–600m may work better than an all-out surge at 1km to go.

Does this advice apply to road and track 5K?

The pacing principles are similar, but hills, road turns, GPS error and crowding can make road-race kilometre targets less uniform than track splits.

10K PACING GUIDE · RESEARCH + RACE STRATEGY

How to pace a 10K

The 10K rewards disciplined pacing: a sustainable opening, patient work through the middle, and a calculated increase in effort when the finish becomes real.

Published 16 October 20268 min readTrack & roadResearch-backed

The quick answer

  1. 0–2km: Settle at your realistic goal pace. The breathing should feel purposeful rather than frantic. Resist the urge to bank time just because the opening feels easy.
  2. 2–5km: Keep cadence and posture relaxed. Check effort against recent race fitness, not the speed of an ambitious group. At 5km you should be committed but not desperate.
  3. 5–7km: This is where concentration matters most. Fatigue increases while the finish is still distant. Hold effort and avoid drifting slower without noticing.
  4. 7–9km: If the plan is working, bring your focus forward. Lift effort gradually instead of making a single costly sprint. Try to sustain pace even as it feels harder.
  5. 9–10km: With the finish in range, commit to your best sustainable speed. A final 400m kick can be effective if you have held something in reserve.

03 / 5–7km

Make the middle count.

This is where concentration matters most. Fatigue increases while the finish is still distant. Hold effort and avoid drifting slower without noticing.

04 / 7–9km

Increase the pressure progressively.

If the plan is working, bring your focus forward. Lift effort gradually instead of making a single costly sprint. Try to sustain pace even as it feels harder.

05 / 9–10km

Race the final kilometre.

With the finish in range, commit to your best sustainable speed. A final 400m kick can be effective if you have held something in reserve.

What does the research actually show?

A study of 394 elite 5000m and 10,000m championship performances compared kilometre splits and recent best times. The authors found differing pacing patterns by event and placing, with tactical positioning playing an important role in medal contention. A separate championship analysis found negative splitting common among medallists, in races where finishing position, not a personal-best time, was the primary objective.

The research also includes recreational athletes: a study of 39 experienced recreational 10K runners examined pacing strategies and heart-rate responses, highlighting that the pacing demands cannot simply be reduced to the strategy of a world-record holder.

THE EVIDENCE IN CONTEXT

Research on Olympic and World Championship 5000m/10,000m races documents substantial pace changes and fast final sections, particularly in medal-winning tactical races. This should not be mistaken for evidence that a runner chasing a road 10K personal best should deliberately run the opening half slowly.

Review: Pacing profiles and tactical behaviors of elite runners ↗

Learning from elite racing

Record attempts versus championship finals. Historical analyses of 10,000m world-record performances have found comparatively consistent middle kilometres and a quicker finish. Championship finals, meanwhile, can involve shifting packs and decisive changes of pace. For a runner chasing sub-40 or sub-50 on the road, the more useful starting template is a sustainable average pace with room for a late push.

Common 10K pacing questions

Should I run a 10K negative split?

A small negative split can be a reasonable outcome if the target pace is conservative enough to hold. It is not compulsory. Avoid deliberately giving away large amounts of time early when racing for a personal best.

How should a sub-40 10K feel halfway?

The average is 4:00 per kilometre. At 5km a roughly 20:00 split is consistent with that plan. Effort should feel hard but sustainable; if you are already sprinting to hold pace, adjust.

When is the hardest part of a 10K?

Many runners report the middle kilometres as mentally demanding, but experiences vary. Focus on rhythm and maintaining form, rather than treating discomfort as a signal to surge.

Should I match pace on hills?

On a hilly course, maintain a broadly sustainable effort instead of forcing identical kilometre times up and down each gradient.

HALF MARATHON PACING GUIDE · 21.0975 KM

How to pace a half marathon

Twenty-one kilometres is long enough for early mistakes to become expensive, but short enough to reward a committed, controlled race. Here's how to start sensibly, settle into rhythm and finish with intent.

Published 16 October 202610 min readRoad · 13.1 milesResearch-backed

The quick answer

  1. 0–3km: Get up to goal pace without overreacting to the crowd. Let your breathing settle and avoid banking time.
  2. 3–8km: Establish a controlled rhythm. Use the overall effort, not the watch alone, especially on hills and windy stretches.
  3. 8–15km: Protect the pace through the mentally demanding middle. Take fuel or fluid according to what you practised, and avoid unnecessary surges.
  4. 15–19km: Reassess honestly. If you still feel in control, begin increasing commitment gradually.
  5. 19km–finish: Commit with what remains. Keep posture and cadence purposeful, then accelerate only if you genuinely can.

Should you run even or negative splits?

For a relatively flat half marathon, a broadly even-pace plan is a practical starting point when chasing a personal best. A small negative split can work when you've judged the target accurately, but deliberately losing substantial time early may also compromise your finish. Think controlled first kilometres, consistent middle, competitive finish rather than an inflexible split formula.

A 2024 systematic review covering 39 studies found that half-marathon participants generally demonstrated less pacing variation than full-marathon runners. A 2026 narrative review drawing on 37 studies also reported generally more even pacing in half marathons than marathons. These are observational findings: they describe what runners have done, and do not establish one optimal pacing pattern for everyone. [1, 2]

Five sections, one controlled race plan

01 / 0–3KM

Don't win the first kilometre.

Adrenaline can make a pace well beyond your fitness feel effortless. Build into your target pace, find clear running room, and avoid weaving through groups. An opening kilometre slightly off the exact target isn't a crisis; a sustained over-fast start can be.

Cue: “Relax, settle, don't chase.”
02 / 3–8KM

Find your rhythm.

Work towards consistent effort. Keep your shoulders loose, your stride natural and your mind calm. Use course markers as well as GPS, which can be noisy in crowded urban races. On climbs or into headwinds, protect effort rather than forcing an exact split.

Cue: “Smooth is fast.”
03 / 8–15KM

Race the middle honestly.

This is often where concentration slips: you are well beyond the start, but the finish still feels distant. Break the section into 2–3km checkpoints. Follow your practised hydration/carbohydrate plan when appropriate for your duration and conditions.

Cue: “One checkpoint at a time.”
04 / 15–19KM

Increase commitment—not panic.

Make a deliberate assessment of breathing, form and remaining energy. If you've paced accurately, try lifting the effort gradually; if struggling, focus on limiting the slowdown instead of forcing an unsustainable sprint.

Cue: “Build pressure.”
05 / 19KM–FINISH

Finish the work.

Keep looking ahead, use your arms and stay purposeful through the final turns. In the last kilometre, commit to whatever faster pace you can sustain without losing form. A strong finish is earned by the decisions you made earlier.

Cue: “Commit, then empty the tank.”

What the evidence says

RESEARCH, NOT A UNIVERSAL RECIPE

Half marathons are generally more evenly paced than marathons. A 2024 systematic review reported greater pacing variability and more slowing over the full marathon than over the half marathon. A 2026 synthesis reached a similar conclusion across performance levels, although the observational evidence has limitations. [1, 2]

A 2026 systematic review and network meta-analysis of experimental endurance studies found no clear overall performance advantage for one imposed pacing strategy over self-selected pacing across its included events. This isn't half-marathon-specific evidence, but reinforces the need to avoid claiming one precise split pattern is scientifically best for everyone. [3]

What an elite race can teach us

At the 2026 Lisbon Half Marathon, Jacob Kiplimo ran a World Athletics-recognised world record of 57:20. He and a rival reached 15km in 40:52, then Kiplimo ran the next 5km in 13:31 and pushed on towards the finish. This is a documented example of late-race commitment at elite level, not a pacing target to copy directly. [4]

Fueling, terrain and weather

Fuel according to race duration, not simply distance. A runner finishing near an hour has different needs from someone racing for two hours or longer. For events lasting around 1–2.5 hours, sports nutrition guidance commonly discusses approximately 30–60g of carbohydrate per hour, provided it is practised and tolerated. This is general guidance, not an instruction to take fuel you have never tried in training. [5]

In hot conditions, lower your opening effort and use conditions—not an idealised pace chart—to guide adjustments. On a hilly route, match effort on the climbs and regain rhythm gradually on flatter sections rather than forcing every kilometre to be identical.

Four pacing mistakes to avoid

  1. Banking time early: a first 3km well faster than current fitness is a risk, not a bonus.
  2. Racing the GPS watch: tunnels, crowds, bends and buildings can distort instant pace. Check kilometre markers and effort.
  3. Ignoring course conditions: adjust effort on climbs and into wind instead of forcing exact even splits.
  4. Waiting too long to focus: don't treat 10–18km as an empty middle section. Break it into checkpoints and stay engaged.

Half marathon pacing FAQs

Should I aim for even or negative splits?

Broadly even pacing is a straightforward starting point for a flat course and a realistic target. A modest negative split can be sensible if you're feeling controlled, but neither strategy guarantees the fastest outcome.

What pace is a 1:45 half marathon?

Approximately 4:59 per kilometre or 8:00 per mile. The interactive planner above calculates precise checkpoints using 21.0975km.

Should I sprint at 15km?

Usually not all-out. Gradually increase effort if you're feeling strong, then commit more decisively in the last kilometres.

Do I need gels in a half marathon?

That depends on duration, nutrition before the race and individual tolerance. Practise any fueling plan in training; avoid trying unfamiliar products on race day.

MARATHON PACING GUIDE · 42.195 KM

How to pace a marathon

The fastest first 10km rarely wins your personal-best marathon. Learn how to choose a sustainable pace, fuel before fatigue builds, and make better decisions when the race becomes difficult after 30km.

Published 16 October 202612 min readRoad · 26.2 milesResearch & real race splits

The quick answer

  1. 0–10km: Start at a realistic, controlled effort. Avoid banking time with a much faster first 5km.
  2. 10–21.1km: Find an efficient rhythm and stick to your planned fuelling, not just your watch.
  3. 21.1–30km: Keep your effort measured. Check conditions and resist the temptation to surge because you feel good.
  4. 30–40km: Focus on maintaining mechanics, fuelling if tolerated, and managing each kilometre as fatigue rises.
  5. Final 2.195km: Only lift the effort decisively if you can still sustain your running form.

Is even pacing best for a marathon?

For a road marathon with a reasonably flat course and favourable conditions, even effort and broadly even splits are a sensible starting point for many runners. A modest negative split—running the second half a little faster—can be an option when the target is conservative and conditions cooperate. That is different from promising that everyone should accelerate in the final 10km.

In a 2024 systematic review covering 39 studies across marathon, half-marathon and ultra-distance competition, positive pacing was frequently observed. Observation is not prescription: the fact that runners often slow down does not prove that planning to slow down is optimal. [1]

A separate study of recreational 10km and marathon runners also observed second-half slowdowns in its samples. This strengthens the case for avoiding an overly ambitious first half, rather than aiming for an exact universal positive- or negative-split percentage. [2]

How to race each section

01 / 0–10KM

Start with restraint.

The crowds and adrenaline can make your goal pace feel unusually easy. Treat this as a reason to stay disciplined, not to accelerate. Settle towards your planned pace, find space, and avoid repeated weaving that adds distance. If the course begins downhill, resist the urge to chase a quick split at the expense of your legs.

Cue: “Let the race come to me.”
02 / 10–21.1KM

Build a rhythm, not a lead.

Stay economical: relaxed shoulders, controlled cadence and small course-aware adjustments. Check effort as well as GPS pace, especially into wind or over hills. Follow the fuelling routine you practised in training; don't wait for a clear energy crash.

Cue: “Smooth, steady, fuel early.”
03 / 21.1–30KM

Hold the plan when it still feels good.

Halfway is not permission to launch a long surge. Reassess the weather, whether you've taken enough carbohydrate, and whether your effort is still sustainable. If your target was realistic, the simplest valuable decision may be to continue doing exactly what you're doing.

Cue: “Patient now, strong later.”
04 / 30–40KM

Protect the second half.

This is where a too-fast start may catch up with you. Think in manageable checkpoints, keep the arms purposeful and avoid tensing your upper body. If speed drops, evaluate effort and symptoms rather than blindly forcing the watch back to its original pace.

Cue: “Stay tall. One kilometre at a time.”
05 / 40–42.195KM

Spend what's left—if it's safe.

If you still have good mechanics and control, increase effort through the final kilometres. Finishing strongly may mean accelerating, or simply losing less speed than expected. There is no need to save a dramatic sprint if the final 2km is already taking everything you have.

Cue: “Keep moving. Finish the job.”

What Kipchoge's 2022 Berlin marathon tells us

Eliud Kipchoge ran 2:01:09 in Berlin in 2022, a world record at the time. World Athletics reported a 59:50 first half. His remaining half therefore took 61:19. Even one of the sport's greatest marathon performances included a positive split. [3]

What this does—and doesn't—prove: Kipchoge's extraordinary performance shows that a positive split can coexist with a world-class result. It does not mean a recreational runner should imitate his aggressive opening pace. Your best strategy depends on fitness, fuelling, terrain and conditions.

Fuel from the beginning—not just when you fade

Marathon pacing and fuelling are inseparable. If you run too hard early, you may burn through carbohydrate faster than planned; if you underfuel, keeping the same pace later can become increasingly difficult. Practice carbohydrate intake in long training runs, including how often you take it and how you tolerate fluids or gels.

The World Athletics 2019 nutrition consensus lists roughly 30–60g carbohydrate per hour for events of 1–2.5 hours, and for events lasting longer than 2.5 hours considers higher intakes, up to around 90g/hour, where appropriate and practised. Individual needs vary; higher isn't automatically better if it causes stomach problems. [4]

Simple fuelling decisions for race day

  • Practise your gel, drink and carbohydrate schedule during long runs—never experiment with large amounts for the first time on race morning.
  • Have a plan for aid stations and know whether your chosen gels require additional water.
  • Adjust fluid intake to conditions and personal sweat losses; avoid both substantial dehydration and overdrinking.
  • Carbohydrate targets are guidance, not a medical prescription. Seek individual advice if you have a medical condition or a history of severe gastrointestinal issues.

Why the weather can change your perfect pace

A target that is realistic on a cool, flat morning may not be realistic in heat, humidity or substantial headwinds. An analysis of results from multiple major US marathons found performance worsened as conditions became hotter, with meaningful variation across runner ability. [5]

On hot days, pace by sustainable effort rather than blindly forcing cool-weather splits. Uphills and wind may require a slower kilometre; downhills can allow faster splits without extra effort, but excessive downhill aggression can strain your legs. Smart marathon pacing adapts to the course instead of fighting it.

Four common marathon pacing mistakes

  1. Banking time in the first 10km: it can feel easy, but the bill often arrives later.
  2. Choosing a goal from hope rather than training: use recent races and sustained marathon-specific training to set a realistic target.
  3. Ignoring fuelling until halfway: a rushed recovery from poor early intake may be harder than steady intake throughout.
  4. Letting GPS dictate every second: turns, tall buildings, hills and crowded sections make pace readings noisy. Use effort and official course markers too.

Marathon pacing questions

Should I aim for a negative split in a marathon?

A slight negative split can be a useful plan when your target is realistic, but it is not mandatory. A broadly even effort is often a safer principle than forcing an ambitious acceleration late in the race.

How fast should I run the first 5km?

Close to a sustainable planned average pace, with a sensible allowance for congestion, terrain and adrenaline. Avoid treating early kilometres as an opportunity to gain several minutes.

Why do runners slow after 30km?

Fatigue is multi-factorial: energy availability, prior pacing, musculoskeletal fatigue, thermal stress and terrain can all matter. There is no single universal “wall” distance.

Should I follow pace or heart rate?

Use both as context rather than obeying either one blindly. Heart rate can drift as heat and duration increase, while instant GPS pace can fluctuate. Practised race effort and reliable course splits remain important.
