800M PACING GUIDE · ATHLETE-INFORMED

How to pace an 800m

Four 200-metre sections. A fast-but-controlled opening. A patient middle. Then the commitment to hold your speed when it hurts. Here’s how to put together an 800m that lasts the full two laps.

Published 16 October 202610 min readTrack / 2 lapsResearch + race examples

The quick answer

  1. 0–200m: Get out decisively, find position and merge after the breakline without wasting energy.
  2. 200–400m: Settle into smooth, fast running. Relax your face and shoulders; don’t force the pace.
  3. 400–600m: Reassess your position at the bell. With 300m remaining, progressively increase effort and stay engaged.
  4. 600–800m: Commit at 200m to go, protect your technique and give everything in the final 100m.

The 800m, broken into four 200s

The following framework comes from the experience of a competitive middle-distance runner, refined against race analysis and published evidence. It gives you mental jobs for every quarter of the race without pretending every 800m should follow exactly the same pace curve. Championship races can demand very different decisions from paced time trials.

01 / 0–200m

Strong start. Smart position.

Accelerate positively, but don't sprint blindly. Get into a useful position while staying relaxed enough to keep moving well. In a standard two-lap 800m, stay in your lane until the marked breakline after the first bend, then merge smoothly towards the inside if it is safe. Taking the shortest line helps, but forcing a gap or getting boxed in can cost you more.

Cue: “Fast, comfortable, controlled.”
02 / 200–400m

Find flow. Don't force it.

Once the field settles, stop fighting the clock and find your rhythm. Keep your stride quick and fluid, the arms efficient, and your upper body relaxed. Resist small surges caused only by anxiety. You can run hard without feeling as if you are forcing every stride.

Cue: “Smooth and tall.”
03 / 400–600m

Prepare, then squeeze.

At the bell, take a quick inventory: are you trapped, on someone's shoulder, or positioned to move? Don't launch an all-out sprint simply because the bell has rung. With about 300m remaining, start building your commitment and defending your position. Make tactical decisions early enough that you're not forced into a desperate wide move.

Cue: “Stay in touch. Start squeezing.”
04 / 600–800m

Gear five. Then gear six.

At 200m to go, commit. Increase your effort, keep the arms active and resist the tendency to tense up. With 100m left, give what remains. Your speed may still fall; the practical goal is often to fade less than the runners around you.

Cue: “Hold form. Fight for every metre.”

One crucial distinction: “Change gears” is a mental instruction, not a guarantee of increasing speed. Research on elite championship middle-distance races suggests that successful athletes often differentiate themselves over the final 200m by slowing less, rather than accelerating dramatically. [3]

Should an 800m be positively split?

Usually, a small positive split is a reasonable starting point for a fast, time-focused 800m: the first 400m is slightly quicker than the second. One athlete-informed target is a second lap approximately 2% slower than the first, but this is an illustrative strategy, not an established physiological optimum.

Observational evidence offers context. A study of 142 elite season-best 800m performances reported that the men's first 200m was the fastest segment and subsequent segments slowed significantly, while women showed a different pattern. A broader review likewise notes that positive pacing is typical of paced 800m meetings, whereas championship races may be positive or negative depending on tactics. [1, 2]

What Rudisha's world record really shows

In the 2012 Olympic final, David Rudisha front-ran a world record of 1:40.91. His official intermediate marks were 49.28 at 400m and 1:14.30 at 600m; race reporting records an opening 200m of approximately 23.4. That produces the approximate segment profile below. [4]

There is no one-to-one link between Rudisha's record splits and the ideal plan for an athlete running 1:55 or 2:20. What the race illustrates is decisive positioning and exceptional speed endurance: a very quick first 200m, continued pressure from 400–600m and a final segment that, despite enormous effort, was slower than his opening section.

What about Coe and Wottle?

Sebastian Coe ran 1:41.73 for an 800m world record in 1981, with approximately 49.7 and 52.1-second laps. Like Rudisha, his record run used a sizeable positive split. Those exceptional performances provide useful reference points, but they don't prove that every runner should go out four or five percent faster. [5]

Dave Wottle's 1972 Olympic victory provides a different perspective. Wottle finished in 1:45.86 after running close to even 200m segments and catching runners who had gone out faster. In a later interview, he explained that his approach was not a rigidly pre-planned trick: it reflected his conditioning, mindset and a relatively tactical race. [6]

Time trial versus championship: If your priority is a personal best with a pacemaker, controlled positive splitting may make sense. If you're racing to qualify or win, changing pace to stay out of trouble or cover a move may matter more than following exact lap targets. [2, 3]

Why the final 200m feels so hard

The 800m demands very high running speeds for long enough that fatigue builds rapidly. People often blame lactic acid for the heavy-legged sensation, but this description isn't scientifically accurate. Lactate isn't simply toxic waste: it can be used as a fuel and it is not the direct, singular cause of fatigue.

Loughborough University exercise physiologist Dr Mark Burnley has explained that fatigue during events such as the 800m and mile involves multiple high-intensity metabolic factors, including inorganic phosphate. The practical point remains: early pace affects how well you can maintain force, coordination and form later. [7]

Three mistakes to avoid

  • Going out like it's a 400m: a fast start is not permission to sprint uncontrollably. Arrive at 200m in a position you can sustain.
  • Checking out from 200 to 500m: relaxing your body is good; losing contact or drifting unnecessarily is not.
  • Waiting until the straight to make a decision: many races are shaped from 500–650m. Anticipate changes rather than reacting once a gap has formed.

Make a plan, then adapt to the race

Use the split explorer to choose a realistic opening lap, and then rehearse your four race cues in training. Know where your target pace should put you through 200m and halfway—but don't treat those numbers as an instruction to ignore the runners around you.

For a race with a pacemaker, focus on staying relaxed within your plan. For an untimed championship, position and the ability to react matter. In both situations, the best finish usually starts with not wasting energy in the opening 400m.

Questions runners ask

Quick answers to common questions about pacing a 800m race.

Should I run an 800m even or positive splits?

Many fast 800m performances use positive splits, but tactical races can be different. Start with a controlled plan based on your own training and adjust to the situation; no single percentage is universally optimal.

Should I kick at 300m or 200m to go?

At about 300m to go, assess position and begin increasing commitment. The final 200m is often about protecting form and resisting deceleration; a major early sprint can backfire if you have too little left.

When can you move into lane 1 in an 800m?

In a standard two-lap track 800m started in lanes, athletes typically break after the first bend at the designated breakline. Always follow the markings and rules for the specific race format. [8]
