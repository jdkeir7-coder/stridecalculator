5K PACING GUIDE · RESEARCH + RACE STRATEGY

How to pace a 5K

A controlled opening, an honest middle, and a committed finish. Learn how to distribute effort through 5km without wasting your race in the first kilometre.

Published 16 October 20268 min readTrack & roadResearch-backed

The quick answer

  1. 0–1km: Accelerate positively, but settle close to your realistic target pace by the first few hundred metres. The aim is to avoid a large early pace spike.
  2. 1–2km: Relax your hands and shoulders, use economical strides, and avoid surges to cover a few positions. Keep the pace honest, not desperate.
  3. 2–3km: This middle kilometre can feel mentally long. Stay focused on form and breathing; don’t mistake rising effort for a need to slow.
  4. 3–4km: Reassess your position and energy. If you feel capable, gradually increase pressure without committing to a full sprint too early.
  5. 4–5km: Stay strong through the penultimate bend or last road turns. With 400–600m left, let the effort rise; use the last 200m for whatever kick remains.

Five kilometres, five clear jobs.

The segments below are a practical framework for a runner trying to run their best time on a broadly flat course. They are not a rigid prescription for every athlete. Hills, wind, competition and current fitness can all alter a sensible split.

What does the research actually show?

In analyses of historical men’s 5000m world records, the first and final kilometres tended to be quicker than the middle sections, while much of the race remained relatively steady. That is a description of world-record behaviour, not proof that recreational runners should start faster than target pace.

Championship 5000m finals can be very different. A peer-reviewed analysis of official Olympic and World Championship splits found fluctuating speed and fast closing phases among medallists and other high finishers. A sensible personal-best plan may therefore look steadier than a championship-winning plan.

THE EVIDENCE IN CONTEXT

World-record pacing and championship racing do not look identical. The published literature describes broadly steady kilometre splits with an end spurt in record races, but championship finals are more variable and often accelerate sharply late. Choose the plan that suits your actual goal.

Learning from elite racing

Joshua Cheptegei, Monaco 2020. His then-world-record 5000m of 12:35.36 offers a compelling example of rhythm: World Athletics reports 1000m in 2:31.87, 2000m in 5:03.77, 3000m in 7:35.14 and a slightly quicker 2:30 fourth kilometre before a powerful final kilometre and 59.64-second final lap. The lesson is disciplined pacing with a reserve for the finish, not blindly replicating elite speeds.

Mistakes that cost runners time

  • Turning the opening 400m into a sprint; the adrenaline can hide an unsustainable pace.
  • Chasing a watch reading on tight bends or under trees; use course markers and perceived effort.
  • Treating the first difficult kilometre as failure and giving up before the finish.
  • Waiting until the final 100m to commit when you still have energy left.

Common 5K pacing questions

Is even pacing best for a 5K?

Even pace is a useful starting point for a personal-best attempt on a relatively flat course. A small controlled quickening late in the race is often preferable to banking time early; championship tactics can be different.

Should my first kilometre be my fastest?

Not deliberately. An opening kilometre that is slightly quick can occur, but accelerating far beyond sustainable pace creates avoidable risk. Aim to settle into the effort you can hold.

When should I kick in a 5K?

There is no universal distance. If you still have control at 4km, build pressure gradually; a committed final 400–600m may work better than an all-out surge at 1km to go.

Does this advice apply to road and track 5K?

The pacing principles are similar, but hills, road turns, GPS error and crowding can make road-race kilometre targets less uniform than track splits.

Research and original data sources

The published findings describe observed behaviour. Our step-by-step race advice is a practical interpretation, not an experimentally proven universal optimum.

  2. Hanley et al. (2020), The Science Behind Competition and Winning in Athletics — Analysis of official championship splits for 507 distance-race performances.
  3. Filipas, La Torre & Hanley, Pacing Profiles of Olympic and World Championship Long-Distance Runners — 394 runners in five 5000m/10,000m championships.
